# The Hollowmere Valley Almanac, 2024 Edition

Compiled by the Office of Small Statistics, Thistledown. Figures are given as recorded by the village clerks; no responsibility is taken for miscounted gnomes, mislabelled mushrooms, or dragons who refused to be measured.

## Foreword

Hollowmere Valley sits in the crook between the Grey Hills and the river Pibble, a place so thoroughly surrounded by scenery that visitors often forget to look at the inhabitants. This almanac exists to correct that oversight. It has been published every year since the Great Teapot Shortage, and in that time it has grown from a single folded leaflet into the document you now hold, which is still mostly leaflet but with better margins.

The Office of Small Statistics employs three clerks, one apprentice, and a heron named Gerald who is responsible for checking the river-level gauges and is, by general agreement, the most reliable member of staff. Readers who find errors in the tables below are asked to write to the Office, preferably in ink, and preferably not on the back of the Office's own stationery again.

## Part I — Census

The midsummer census is carried out on the longest evening of the year, when everybody is outside anyway and the counting can be done over a pleasant glass of elderflower cordial. Each village sends its own clerk, who stands on a crate and counts heads; the three tallies are then reconciled at the Thistledown Reading Room, usually with some shouting. The figures below cover fifteen years and show the steady, if uneven, growth of the valley.

Pebblewick deserves special mention. Founded within living memory by a family of cheese-makers who took a wrong turn on the way to somewhere else, it has been the fastest-growing village in the valley every single year of the survey. The clerks attribute this to the new bakery, the mild microclimate, and an unspoken rule that anyone who stays for a second slice of cake is considered resident.

Thistledown, by contrast, has had a bumpier decade. The population dipped in a few years when a cohort of young gnomes left to seek their fortunes in the Floating Market, though most of them returned within a season, citing the price of lodgings and the absence of proper bread. Mossbury has remained broadly stable, which its inhabitants describe as 'character' and its neighbours describe as 'stubbornness'.

## Part II — Harvest

No almanac of the valley would be complete without the Whispering Wood, which is both its main source of income and its chief source of unexplained noises. Licensed foragers are permitted to gather mushrooms between dawn and the second bell, and must report their baskets to the Mossbury weighing-house. The woodland itself takes no position on licensing, but is known to hide the best chanterelles from foragers it dislikes.

Readers with a head for economics will notice that the price per kilogram falls as the harvest rises. This is the oldest law in the valley and predates the Office of Small Statistics by several centuries. The weighing-house clerks have long suspected that a few enterprising foragers hold baskets back in the glut months and release them in the lean ones, but since nobody has ever been caught, the practice is officially known as 'prudent storage'.

Rainfall, as might be expected, drives the whole cycle. A wet spring is followed by a spectacular chanterelle season, and a wet autumn by a respectable showing of porcini. The year 2023 was considered average, which is to say that everybody complained about it, but only mildly.

## Part III — Dragons

The Hollowmere caves have been home to dragons for as long as anyone has bothered to write it down, which is not very long, as dragons tend to eat the people who ask too many questions. Relations improved considerably after the Treaty of the Long Meadow, under which the dragons agreed to stop burning the hay, and the villages agreed to provide a regular supply of snacks. The Draconic Register is updated each spring, with measurements taken by volunteers who are, on the whole, very brave.

| Ember | 14 | 3.9 | 92 | toasted marshmallows |

| Marigold | 45 | 7.5 | 133 | smoked eel |
| Vesper | 63 | 8.6 | 146 | gingerbread |
| Pudding | 19 | 4.2 | 88 | pudding |

A pattern emerges from the data: wingspan and speed both increase with age up to a point, after which they level off and then decline. Old Tallow, at over two centuries, is the largest dragon in the valley by a comfortable margin, but also the slowest to take off, and has been known to request a running start and a tailwind. Young Nib is the fastest per year of age and is currently the subject of a friendly wager among the Pebblewick bakers.

The snacks column is, strictly speaking, outside the Office's remit, but the dragons insisted. Pudding's choice is considered by the clerks to be either extremely literal or an act of quiet defiance; the matter has not been pursued.

## Afterword

The Office wishes to thank Gerald the heron, the volunteers who held the stopwatches, and the dragons for their cooperation. Next year's edition will include a section on the Pibble river eels, subject to the eels' agreement.
